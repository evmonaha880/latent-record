what are the timelines of rockafeller, carnegie, pullman, and other titans of industry giving away their fortunes vs. eastman

Claude · 11:33

The pattern is the answer: the titans gave after they finished; Eastman gave while he worked. And almost all of them bought perpetuity — a foundation with their name that outlived them. Eastman refused the foundation entirely, spent it down, and died with the ledger closed. "My work is done" reads differently against this backdrop.

The timelines

Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919). Wrote the theory in 1889 — The Gospel of Wealth, "the man who dies thus rich dies disgraced" — but the giving waited on the exit: he sold Carnegie Steel in 1901 at 65 (~$225M to him personally) and then spent eighteen years giving away ~90% of it. 2,509 libraries, Carnegie Institution (1902), and when he found he couldn't give it away fast enough, he created the Carnegie Corporation (1911) to do it for him — the first perpetual mega-foundation. Preached spend-down, built a perpetuity anyway.

John D. Rockefeller (1839–1937). Tithed from his first paycheck, funded the University of Chicago from 1889, but the system arrived after he left Standard Oil's active management (~1897): General Education Board 1902, Sanitary Commission 1909, Rockefeller Foundation 1913 — the perpetual, professionally staffed, dynastic model, run by Frederick Gates and Junior. Gave ~$540M over a lifetime, most of it into endowments designed to never end.

George Pullman (1831–1897). The inverse, and the one that pays off hardest against Eastman. His "philanthropy" was the company town — rent-collecting paternalism that produced the 1894 strike. He died three years later so hated that his family buried him at night under tons of concrete and steel rail, so his own workers couldn't desecrate the grave. Hold that next to your paragraph: Pullman's grave was fortified against the factory. Eastman had his ashes placed at the factory. Same gesture, opposite meanings — that's a book beat, maybe an essay beat.

The kindred spirits (the two worth adding to your frame):
- Julius Rosenwald (Sears, 1862–1932 — died the same year as Eastman). The real intellectual twin: militant "giving while living," explicitly anti-perpetuity — the Rosenwald Fund had a sunset clause and spent itself out of existence by 1948. And his signature program was the Rosenwald schools with Tuskegee, the same institution Eastman gave a million to in 1924.
- Milton Hershey (1857–1945). The anonymity twin: secretly signed over essentially his whole fortune to the Hershey Industrial School trust in 1918 — it stayed unknown for five years until the NYT found it in 1923. Mr. Smith had company.

The rest, quickly: Ford gave little in life; the Ford Foundation (1936) was substantially an estate-tax structure that became huge after his death — posthumous by design. Mellon's National Gallery came in 1936–37, at the end. Vanderbilt gave ~$1M of a $100M fortune and kept the rest dynastic. Russell Sage gave nothing; his widow built the foundation from his estate in 1907.

Where Eastman breaks the pattern

  • Mid-career, not post-exit. The anonymous $2.5M to MIT is 1912 — he's 57, twenty years from death, running the company at its height. Carnegie at the equivalent career moment was still crushing the Homestead strike.
  • No foundation. Ever. No Eastman Foundation exists. Carnegie and Rockefeller converted money into perpetual institutions bearing their names; Eastman put money into operating institutions — a medical school, a dental clinic, a music school — mostly bearing other purposes, sometimes bearing no name at all.
  • Spend-down to zero. He gave the bulk away alive (commonly put north of $100M), handed employees a slab of his own stock in 1919, and the will was signed ninety minutes before the pistol. The giving and the life ended on the same schedule — which is exactly the "meticulous operator closes the ledger" reading your death scene already carries.
  • Anonymity as policy, not modesty — you already have his own sentence for why.

The Pullman concrete-grave fact and the Rosenwald sunset clause are the two I'd verify by lane before either enters sendable text — both are famous, neither is yet sourced to our standard.

Claude · 11:54

Three readings of what "the work" was in March 1932 — they're genuinely different claims, so pick by what you want the death to mean.

Option A — the bookkeeper's close. The work was the ledger, and he audited it.

The note was seven words. My work is done. Why wait?

It was a bookkeeper's sentence, and he knew exactly what stood on the books. An idea: that anyone could make a photograph. A machine for keeping the promise, thirteen hundred acres of it. A laboratory that would deliver the color he had chased for thirty years, three years too late for him to see it. A city with his name over its doors, and a school in Boston without it. There was no heir on the ledger, because the ledger was the heir. He had audited his own life the way he had audited everything since he was nineteen, found the accounts in balance, and closed them.

Option B — he was right. The work was the promise on the box, and the promise had been kept.

The note was seven words. My work is done. Why wait?

By his own definition, the claim was true. The work had never been the company. It was the promise on the box: you press the button, we do the rest. In 1877 a photograph had required a wagonload of glass and chemistry and a tent. By 1932 the camera was in the house, the album was on the shelf, and pressing the button was something a child did without thinking. The customer he invented had become nearly everyone. What remained, running the company, defending the margins, riding out the Depression, was other men's work, of a kind he had spent thirty years training them to do. The thing only he could have done was finished.

Option C — he was wrong. The work had a second life he couldn't see, and the flip hands straight into the next paragraph.

The note was seven words. My work is done. Why wait?

He was wrong, and wrong in a way only he could be. If the work was a camera company, it stood that spring in the worst year of the Depression. If the work was the thing he actually built, the habit of believing what you have seen a picture of, it was barely leaving the ground: the color was three years away, the war that photography would help win was seven, the moon was thirty-four. The man who measured everything had, at the last, measured only his own share of it. The work was not done. It had simply stopped requiring him.

My call: A. The bookkeeper motif is now load-bearing in your sixth pass — "keeping books for seven hundred dollars a year," "the bookkeeper understood business far better than he ever understood chemistry" — so a bookkeeper's death sentence closes a loop the essay has already opened, and "the ledger was the heir" does the no-family work without repeating the grave paragraph.

The real competition is C, because "It had simply stopped requiring him" is a perfect handoff into the very next line — "The company ran another eighty years on his instructions" — and it makes the death the hinge of the essay's whole second half. If you want the death scene to close the man, take A. If you want it to open the back half, take C. B is the gentlest and the most defensible historically, but it does the least work.

(A and C can't both live — they contradict each other on purpose. And note C's "war that photography would help win was seven" counts to 1939; if you'd rather count to Pearl Harbor it's nine.)
